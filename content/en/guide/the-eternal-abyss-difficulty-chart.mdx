## What the Difficulty Chart Actually Measures in The Eternal Abyss

If you have ever loaded into The Eternal Abyss, picked a tower that looked reasonable, and gotten flattened in the first minute, you already understand why players obsess over the difficulty chart. The eternal abyss difficulty chart is the shared reference the community uses to decide what to attempt next, and it exists because the game gives you almost no handholding. Community reports describe The Eternal Abyss as a deliberately punishing experience where losing progress and getting frustrated are expected parts of the climb.

That design choice is exactly why the chart matters. Without it, you are guessing — and guessing in a game that punishes guesswork is how you burn an evening on content you were never ready for.

The chart is not a single number slapped on a tower. It blends three separate signals that experienced players read together:

- **A numeric rating** that places a structure on a long scale, often running past 10.
- **A named tier** such as Terrifying, Catastrophic, Insane, or Unreal, which groups similar structures together.
- **The structure type**, because a citadel and a tower with the same rating do not demand the same stamina.

Understanding all three is what separates a productive session from a rage-quit.

### Why the Chart Keeps Changing

One thing newcomers rarely realize: the chart is living documentation. The Eternal Abyss includes difficulty chart displays in its world, and the community wiki records multiple revisions of those charts over time — an original version, a third, a sixth, an eighth, a tenth, and beyond. Ratings get re-evaluated as players improve, as routes are optimized, and as new structures are added.

That means any number you memorize today may drift tomorrow. Treat the chart as a strong guide, not a permanent verdict.

## Towers, Citadels, and Great Citadels: What the Chart Is Ranking

A frequent mistake is assuming a rating describes raw execution difficulty alone. It does not. Structure type changes how that difficulty is delivered, and the chart accounts for it.

| Structure type | Typical scope | What makes it harder than the rating suggests | Example from community records |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tower | Short, focused climb | Minimal room for error; a single slip can end the run | Tower of Difficulty Chart (Insane) |
| Citadel | Longer multi-section climb | Endurance and route memory matter as much as precision | Citadel of Latest Difficulty Chart |
| Great Citadel | Largest scale | Long sessions, compounding mistakes, heavy progress loss | Great Citadel of Great Difficulty Chart (Unreal) |

Here is the practical takeaway: a 10.85 tower and a 10.85 citadel are not interchangeable. The citadel will test your stamina, your memory, and your patience in ways the tower never will. When you scan the eternal abyss difficulty chart, always read the type column before the number column.

Great Citadels sit in a class of their own. Community records place the Great Citadel of Great Difficulty Chart at Unreal difficulty and describe it as an ascension-based structure located in Timeless Manor. Structures like that are not "next up" content — they are destination content you build toward over many sessions.

## How to Read the Chart Before You Start a Tower

Reading the chart well is a skill. The steps below turn a wall of numbers into a decision you can actually act on.

| Step | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find the structure's current rating and tier name | Gives you a baseline comparison point |
| 2 | Check the structure type (tower, citadel, great citadel) | Adjusts expectations for length and endurance |
| 3 | Look for recent revisions in the chart logs | A rating from two chart versions ago may be stale |
| 4 | Compare against the hardest structure you have already cleared | Anchors an abstract number to real experience |
| 5 | Check whether the structure has a gimmick | Gimmicks change what skills are actually tested |
| 6 | Decide your attempt budget before you load in | Prevents a "one more try" spiral that eats your night |

Step 4 is the one players skip, and it is the most valuable. A rating of 10.90 means nothing in isolation. A rating of 10.90 when your best clear sits at 10.60 means you are looking at a meaningful step up — one that probably deserves practice time, not a blind run.

## Progression Tips, Readiness Checks, and Common Mistakes

Most progression walls in The Eternal Abyss are not skill walls. They are preparation walls. The chart tells you what you are facing; these habits determine whether you survive it.

**Actionable tips that consistently show up in player experience reports:**

- **Climb in tier order, not rating order.** Clearing everything in High-Peak Terrifying before touching Peak Terrifying builds a cleaner skill base than cherry-picking decimals.
- **Re-clear a structure you have already beaten before attempting a new tier.** It confirms your consistency, not just your best attempt.
- **Treat lost progress as tuition.** The game is explicitly designed so that failure costs you; budgeting for that mentally keeps you from tilting.
- **Log your attempts.** Writing down where you died turns a vague "this is impossible" into a specific practice target.
- **Respect band boundaries.** Moving from one named tier into the next is the real difficulty step, not the decimal change.

| Common mistake | What it looks like | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Chasing decimals | Skipping a 10.85 to attempt an 11.01 | Finish the tier you are in first |
| Ignoring structure type | Attempting a great citadel like a short tower | Plan for a long session with breaks |
| Using stale ratings | Trusting a number from an old chart revision | Check the latest chart log |
| No attempt budget | Playing until frustration decides for you | Set a stop point before you start |
| Solo-only mindset | Never comparing notes with other players | Community reports reveal route tricks you will not find alone |

The chart is a map, not a shortcut. Players who treat it as a planning tool progress steadily; players who treat it as a checklist of things to brute-force tend to stall in the same tier for weeks.

## FAQ About the Eternal Abyss Difficulty Chart

**Is the eternal abyss difficulty chart official?**

It is best understood as community documentation. The game includes difficulty chart displays in its world, and the community wiki records those charts across multiple revisions. The numbers and tier names you see are maintained by players and reflect consensus rather than a fixed developer constant.

**Why do the ratings have decimals past 10?**

Because the scale needs room to separate structures that are genuinely close in difficulty. A 10.84 and a 10.90 sit in the same named tier for a reason — the gap is small. Band boundaries, like moving from Peak Terrifying to Bottom Catastrophic, matter far more than any individual decimal.

**Does a higher rating always mean harder for me personally?**

No. Structure type, gimmicks, and your own strengths all shift the experience. A gimmick tower that escalates each floor may punish you differently than a long citadel at a similar rating. Use the chart to compare, then adjust based on your own clears.

**What should I attempt after clearing a Peak Terrifying structure?**

Look for the next named band rather than the next decimal. If your best clear sits at Peak Terrifying, a Bottom Catastrophic structure is the natural next challenge — and a great citadel in that band is a multi-session project, not a single evening's attempt.
