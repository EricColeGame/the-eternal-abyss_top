## What Is The Eternal Abyss?

The Eternal Abyss (commonly shortened to TEA) is a tower-styled obby game on Roblox. Rather than one endless course, it hands players a growing library of towers — tall, self-contained obstacle courses that each finish with a win pad at the top. Community wikis describe it as an official-feeling entry in the tower-climbing genre, the same family of games that produced Juke's Towers of Hell and its many fangames.

The design target players cite most often is **50 towers per area**, which gives the game a very long tail. Areas are split by theme and difficulty, so a tower you can clear on your first afternoon might sit a short walk from one that will eat your entire evening.

| Detail | What the community reports |

Those win-rate figures come from third-party stat trackers rather than the developer, so treat them as directional. What they tell you is simple: the top of the difficulty curve is genuinely punishing, and almost nobody clears it casually. That's the core reason tracking is not optional for anyone serious about TEA.

## What an Eternal Abyss Tower Tracker Actually Tracks

A tower tracker is any tool that records what you've completed. In practice, most players use one of three kinds: an in-game progress view, a community wiki with interactive maps and progress tracking, or a dedicated third-party stats site that pulls your Roblox username and lists every tower you've finished.

### The data that actually matters

Good trackers record more than a yes/no completion flag. The useful fields are:

- **Completion status** — beaten, in progress, or untouched
- **Difficulty tier** — the rating assigned by the community or the game itself
- **Attempt count** — how many runs it took, not just whether you eventually won
- **Area grouping** — which zone the tower belongs to, so you can see gaps in a region
- **Date of first clear** — handy for spotting skill jumps over months

### Manual vs. automated tracking

Automated trackers are faster and less error-prone, but they depend on your account being correctly linked. Manual tracking — a spreadsheet, a notes app, or a Discord log — is slower but catches everything automated tools miss, including practice runs and partial attempts that never produced a win.

| Tracker type | Setup effort | Accuracy | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-game progress view | None | High for wins, low for detail | Casual check-ins |
| Community wiki + maps | Low | Medium | Planning a route through an area |
| Third-party stats site | Low (username lookup) | High for completions | Comparing yourself to others |
| Personal spreadsheet | High | Total control | Speedrunners and completionists |

Most TEA players end up layering two tools: one for verification and one for planning. A stats site confirms what you've beaten; a wiki map tells you where the remaining towers physically sit in the world.

| Option | What it gives you | Sync model | Watch out for |

## How to Set Up Your Tracker in Five Steps

Setting up takes about ten minutes. Doing it properly saves you dozens of "wait, did I beat that one?" moments later.

| Step | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Choose a primary tracker | One source of truth beats three half-updated lists |
| 2 | Create an account or use guest mode | Cloud sync survives a wiped browser; guest mode doesn't |
| 3 | Link your Roblox username | Unlocks automatic completion imports |
| 4 | Import your history | Establishes a baseline before you climb anything new |
| 5 | Add a backup layer | A spreadsheet or screenshots protect you if a tool shuts down |

**Player experience note:** several community trackers let you log in with Discord or Google for cloud sync, or continue as a guest to keep data locally. Cloud sync is the better choice if you play across more than one device, because guest data simply doesn't travel.

### Step 4 in more detail

Don't skip the import. A tracker with an empty history is just a to-do list. Once your completed towers are loaded, you can sort by area, filter by difficulty, and see exactly how many of a zone's 50 towers remain.

## Reading Your Stats Like a Pro

Raw numbers are only useful if you know what to do with them. Here's how experienced players interpret the columns on a typical tracker dashboard.

| Metric | What it really tells you | How to act on it |
|---|---|---|
| Total completions | Your breadth of experience | Aim to fill gaps in one area at a time |
| Win rate on a single tower | Whether you're ready or grinding blindly | Under ~10% suggests you should practice sections first |
| Difficulty tier | Community consensus on the challenge | Climb one tier at a time, not two |
| Attempts per tower | Your personal consistency | Rising attempts on easy towers means fatigue, not weakness |
| Area progress (x/50) | How close a zone is to finished | Target near-complete zones to bank momentum |
| First-clear dates | Long-term improvement curve | Compare months, not days |

**Community reports** suggest the hardest listed towers sit near a 0.1% win rate. If you're beating anything in that band, you're in rare company — and your tracker is the proof.

### Use tiers as a ladder, not a wall

The tower obby genre generally runs from easy introductory climbs up through extreme tiers. The exact naming varies by game and by wiki, so check your tracker's own legend rather than assuming a label means the same thing everywhere.

## Common Tracking Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

| Mistake | Why it hurts | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Only logging wins | Hides progress on towers you're close to beating | Log attempts and best height reached |
| Trusting a single source | One stale tracker creates false gaps | Cross-check with a second tool monthly |
| Tracking hours after a session | Memory blurs, entries get wrong | Log the moment you leave a tower |
| Ignoring area grouping | You end up with 49/50 in five zones | Filter by area and finish one |
| Chasing the hardest tower first | Burns motivation fast | Clear a tier below your ceiling to warm up |

### The two-minute weekly review

Once a week, open your tracker and do three things: confirm recent completions imported, note any tower where your attempts spiked, and pick your next target from the nearest incomplete area. That habit alone keeps a long game like TEA from turning into a shapeless grind.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there an official Eternal Abyss tower tracker built into the game?**
Not in the sense of a full stats dashboard. The game shows your own progress, but detailed per-tower history usually comes from community wikis and third-party tools that read your Roblox username.

**Do I need an account to use a tower tracker?**
No. Most community trackers offer a guest mode that stores data locally. You only need to sign in — often with Discord or Google — if you want cloud sync across devices.

**Can a tower tracker tell me which tower to attempt next?**
Indirectly, yes. Sort by area, filter out completed towers, and pick the highest difficulty you've consistently cleared. That's usually the sweet spot between progress and frustration.

**How accurate are third-party stats for The Eternal Abyss?**
Accuracy is high for completions but can lag after updates. If a tower is missing from your list right after a patch, give the tool time to catch up before assuming you lost progress.
