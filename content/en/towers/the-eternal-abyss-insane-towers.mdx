## Why the Insane Tier Is the Real Progression Wall

Few Roblox climbing games punish optimism quite like this one. The Eternal Abyss Insane towers sit near the top of the game's difficulty ladder, and the gap between them and the tiers below is enormous — one mistimed jump can erase ten minutes of careful progress. This guide breaks down what makes The Eternal Abyss Insane towers so punishing, where they sit in the world, and how to approach your first clear without burning hours on avoidable mistakes.

The game itself is upfront about its tone. Its official description warns that it is a very difficult experience with little-to-no handholding, and that losing progress — and getting frustrated — is simply part of playing it. That framing matters here. Insane isn't a difficulty spike you can brute-force with enthusiasm; it's the point where the game stops forgiving small errors.

Community reports consistently describe the tier this way: lower difficulties teach you individual techniques one at a time, while Insane towers assume you already own those techniques and start testing whether you can repeat them under pressure. The skill ceiling stops being about knowledge and starts being about consistency.

There's also a volume problem. The community-maintained wiki lists roughly 25 Insane towers in the game, which means this tier isn't a single boss you grind until you win — it's a whole catalog of climbs, each with its own gimmick, pacing, and failure points. That's actually good news: you have options. If one tower's style never clicks, another almost certainly will.

| What Insane towers test heavily | What they rarely test |
| --- | --- |
| Consistency across long stretches | Raw reaction speed alone |
| Route knowledge and memorization | Luck |
| Precision on narrow platforms | Grinding for upgrades |
| Patience and tilt control | Inventory or menu management |

## How Insane Fits Into the Difficulty Ladder

The Eternal Abyss sorts its towers by difficulty, and the wiki maintains a dedicated category for the Insane tier. That category page is the single most useful resource before you start, because it tells you how many towers exist at that level and what they're called — the listing includes entries such as Starry Night Tower of Difficulty alongside other high-tier builds.

Difficulty labels aren't just bragging rights. They're a filter. The game's areas are organized so that harder towers are grouped away from the zones where new players spawn, which means the neighborhood a portal sits in is itself a hint about what you're walking into. The main hub area, for example, is documented as hosting towers ranging from Insane upward, while the purple forest section is where the game's Unreal-difficulty towers live — a tier above Insane that serves as a preview of what comes next.

Before you commit to an attempt, read the signals:

| Signal | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| The tower's difficulty category on the wiki | Roughly where it sits relative to everything else |
| Which area the portal is in | Higher tiers are grouped away from starter zones |
| Community completion counts | A rough proxy for popularity and accessibility |
| Footage of the opening section | Whether the first thirty seconds are already above your comfort level |

If the opening of a tower looks hard in a video, it will feel twice as hard in practice. That's not pessimism — it's the honest calibration most players wish they'd done earlier.

## What Actually Makes a Tower "Insane"

Strip away the labels and the Insane tier comes down to a handful of design decisions that stack on top of each other. Player experience points to the same recurring traits: platforms narrow to a single stud, checkpoints stretch further apart, and gimmicks that were introduced individually in lower tiers start appearing in combination.

The camera becomes a real opponent too. Sections built around wrap-around jumps, tight corridors, and moving objects force you to manage your view at the same time as your movement, and that mental load is what separates a Challenging-tier climb from an Insane one.

| Design element | Lower-tier towers | Insane towers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Platform size | Comfortable, forgiving landings | Narrow, sometimes single-stud |
| Checkpoint spacing | Frequent | Sparse and widely separated |
| Gimmicks | Introduced one at a time | Layered and combined |
| Margin for error | Absorbs a bad jump | Frequently zero |
| Section length | Short bursts | Long, sustained sequences |

None of this is random cruelty. The tier exists to test whether your fundamentals hold up when the safety net is removed — and the only way to find out is to try, fail, and note exactly where the failure happened.

## Practical Tips for Clearing The Eternal Abyss Insane Towers

Most failed attempts don't fail at the hardest jump. They fail at the second-hardest jump, three checkpoints in, because the player was already tilted. Here's a workflow that community veterans tend to converge on.

| Step | Action | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Scout before you commit | You cannot solve a route you've never seen |
| 2 | Chunk the tower at checkpoints | Each checkpoint gap becomes a mini-tower you can win |
| 3 | Isolate your failure jump | Practice wrap-arounds, wall-hugs, and truss timing separately |
| 4 | Stabilize your frame rate | Consistent performance matters more than pretty graphics |
| 5 | Stop after three deaths in a row | Tilt is the biggest hidden difficulty modifier in the game |
| 6 | Log where you died | Your death locations are an honest skill map |

### A Quick Readiness Checklist

- You've cleared several towers in the tiers directly below Insane.
- You can name the specific jump type that kills you most often.
- You know where the win pad or finish is before you start climbing.
- You can play for twenty minutes without your hands cramping.
- You're willing to lose an hour and come back tomorrow.

That last point is not a joke. The game's own store page tells players that losing progress is expected — treating a wipe as data rather than a verdict is the single biggest mental shift that separates players who finish Insane towers from players who stall out on them.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Copying a speedrun route without the mechanical skill to execute it.
- Attempting the tier immediately after a frustrating loss in a lower tier.
- Playing on an unstable connection, where input delay silently ruins precise jumps.
- Skipping the wiki entirely and picking a tower at random.
- Restarting instantly after a death instead of pausing to identify the cause.

If you're working through the tier systematically, it helps to keep a short list of three towers you're actively rotating between. Variety keeps your hands sharp and stops one bad route from poisoning your whole session. ## FAQ

**How many Insane towers are in The Eternal Abyss?**
The community wiki currently lists around 25 Insane towers, though that number moves as the game receives updates. Check the Insane category page for the live count before you plan a route through the tier.

**Do I have to beat The Eternal Abyss Insane towers to progress?**
Not strictly. The game is organized into four areas with different difficulty ranges, so you can spend a long time in lower tiers. That said, the Insane tier is where most of the community's attention and prestige lives, and clearing one is a genuine milestone.

**Which Insane tower should a first-timer attempt?**
There's no universal answer, because difficulty within a tier still varies. Player experience suggests picking a tower with short, clearly separated sections, watching a full clear first, and avoiding anything with a reputation for a brutal final stretch.

**How long does a typical Insane clear take?**
It depends far more on the player than the tower. Some veterans finish a route in a single sitting; most players need multiple sessions spread across days. Plan for a learning period rather than a single run, and treat each attempt as reconnaissance.
